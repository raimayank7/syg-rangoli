Rangoli colour combinations

Two people can draw the same design, and one rangoli glows while the other looks flat. The difference is nearly always the choice of colours. You do not need an artist's training for this. A few plain rules, and a handful of ready combinations, are enough.

Rule 1: light next to dark

Colours stand out when a light one sits beside a dark one. Yellow beside orange is two light, warm colours, and from a distance they merge. Yellow beside dark blue, purple or deep green is sharp. Before filling, look at your colours through half-closed eyes: if two neighbours look like one patch, separate them with a line of white or swap one of them.

Rule 2: white does the hard work

White is the most useful colour in the box. A thin white outline around each shape makes every colour brighter and hides uneven edges. White dots give sparkle. A white ring gives the eye a rest between two rings of strong colour. Buy more white than any other colour.

Rule 3: one main colour, one partner, one accent

A design in which every colour is used equally looks restless. Let one colour cover the most area, give a second colour about half as much, and use a third in small touches: dots, centres, tips of petals. Add white, and the design already looks planned.

Rule 4: warm at the centre, cool at the edge (or the other way)

Warm colours, the yellows, oranges, reds and pinks, seem to come forward. Cool colours, the blues, greens and purples, seem to sit back. A yellow centre with warm petals and a cool outer ring looks like a lit lamp. Reversing it, a blue centre with warm outer rays, gives a different, calmer design. Either works. Mixing warm and cool at random in every ring does not.

Rule 5: repeat a colour

Use the colour of the centre again in the outermost dots or the border. The repeat ties the whole design together.

The floor is one of your colours

  • Light floors (white or cream tiles, marble): pale colours vanish. Use strong, dark colours, and outline in a dark colour instead of white, or lay a dark base circle first and build the design on it.
  • Dark floors (grey stone, dark granite, tar): everything shows, and white is brilliant. Light, bright colours look their best here.
  • Red floors (red oxide, terracotta, a geru-washed courtyard): the traditional background. White is superb on it, and so are yellow and green. Red and orange powders disappear into it.
  • Patterned tiles: the pattern fights the rangoli. Lay a plain base of one colour, or make the design on a board, a tray or a plain sheet.

Working with few colours

Two colours and white are enough for a beautiful rangoli. With only two, choose one warm and one cool, such as orange and blue or pink and green, and let white separate them. With one colour, draw a line design in the manner of the white line flower and fill nothing.

Shades from one packet

Mix a colour with white powder or rice flour to get a lighter shade of it. Three shades of one colour, dark at the centre and pale at the edge, make petals look rounded, and they stretch a small packet a long way. Mix in a bowl, a little at a time, and make enough of each shade to finish the ring.

Colours by lamp light

A rangoli made for Diwali evening is seen by the light of diyas and warm bulbs. In that light, yellows and oranges glow, white stays bright, and blues and purples turn dark and dull. If the design will mostly be seen after sunset, lean towards warm colours and plenty of white, and keep blue for small accents.

Try it on paper first

Every design page here has an Outline button and a print option. Print two or three copies and colour them with pencils or crayons in different combinations. Put them on the floor, stand up and look. The one that reads best from standing height is the one to make. It takes ten minutes and saves an hour of regret.

Common colour mistakes

  • Yellow next to white, or light green next to yellow: too close to tell apart.
  • Too many colours in a small design. Three and white is plenty under two feet.
  • Dark outlines around dark fillings: the outline disappears.
  • Different colours in matching positions. If one petal is pink, its opposite number should be too.
